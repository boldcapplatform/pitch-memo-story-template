# Examples: Before and After

Five examples showing what founders commonly do — the Sequoia-era approach that shaped every deck since 2010 — and what the same slide becomes after pitch-story is applied.

The running example is a deliberately generic AI startup: **Corely**, building AI agents for enterprise customer support. Generic on purpose: the old approach makes Corely sound like everyone; the skill makes Corely sound like itself.

---

## 1. The Opinion, Not the Purpose

**Founder's material:** "We need our opening slide."

**❌ The old way (Company Purpose slide)**

> Corely is the AI-powered platform transforming enterprise customer support with intelligent, seamless automation.

**Problems:**

- Every word could belong to forty other companies this quarter. Nothing here is arguable, so nothing is memorable.
- "AI-powered," "transforming," "seamless" — borrowed language, which investors read as borrowed thinking.
- A purpose statement asks for agreement. Agreement is forgotten by the elevator.

**✅ After pitch-story (The World)**

> Support automation fails because it's built to deflect tickets. We think deflection is the wrong goal — a deflected customer is an angry customer with no receipt. Corely's agents are built to *close* issues, and we measure resolution, not deflection.

**What changed:** The interview asked "what do you believe that competent people in support automation would argue with?" The answer divides the room — deflection-metric companies will disagree — and that division is what a partner repeats at Monday's meeting.

---

## 2. The Tribe, Not the TAM

**Founder's material:** "Market slide: the CX software market is $145B."

**❌ The old way (Market Size slide)**

> TAM: $145B global CX market. SAM: $18B enterprise support software. SOM: $400M (capturing just 2%).

**Problems:**

- Every investor has seen this exact slide with different logos. It proves the founder can multiply, not that anyone wants the product.
- "Just 2%" is the oldest tell in venture: a plan reasoned from the market's size, not the customer's behavior.
- Nothing on the slide is evidence. All of it is arithmetic.

**✅ After pitch-story (The Tribe)**

> We haven't done outbound. Eleven of our fourteen customers came from one Reddit thread where a support lead wrote: "first agent that doesn't make my CSAT worse." They resemble each other: mid-market, post-Zendesk, support teams of 15–40 who own their own P&L. That's who's pulling — and there are thousands of them making the same switch this year.

**What changed:** The interview asked "who found you without being sold, and what were their exact words?" The quote is evidence, the resemblance is the market definition, and the pull is the sizing argument. A tribe growing by resemblance is a market being born.

---

## 3. The Decision, Not the Feature Grid

**Founder's material:** "Product slide: our features and our accuracy."

**❌ The old way (Product slide)**

> ✓ Omnichannel support ✓ 50+ integrations ✓ Real-time analytics ✓ Enterprise-grade security. Our proprietary AI is 10x more accurate than competitors.

**Problems:**

- Feature lists converge: every competitor's slide is identical by the third pitch of the day.
- "10x more accurate" — than what, measured how? An unsourced number is worse than no number; it invites the diligence question the founder can't answer.
- Nothing reveals a decision. Features tell what was built, never why or at what cost.

**✅ After pitch-story (deeply technical: the decision, the rejected path, the eval)**

> Everyone builds support agents as RAG over the help center. We tried it for six weeks and rejected it — retrieval answers questions, but resolution requires *acting*: refunds, reschedules, escalations with state. So we built on a typed action graph over the customer's systems, which cost us four months and every quick demo. Then we built a 900-case resolution eval from real closed tickets: we resolve 71% end-to-end; the RAG baseline we abandoned resolves 22%.

**What changed:** One architectural decision with its cost, one rejected reference architecture with its reason, one proprietary eval with an honest number. A technical partner can probe any layer and find the founder home — which is what "product" means now.

## 4. What Can't Be Cloned, Not the 2x2

**Founder's material:** "Competition slide — we're top right."

**❌ The old way (Competition quadrant)**

> [2x2: "Intelligence" vs "Ease of Use." Corely alone in the top right. Six competitor logos scattered bottom-left.]

**Problems:**

- The axes were chosen after the positions. Every founder is top right; every investor knows it.
- It frames competition as today's feature comparison — exactly the frame AI destroys, since features now converge in weeks.
- It answers a question nobody asked, and skips the one everybody did: what happens when a well-funded competitor copies you?

**✅ After pitch-story (What Can't Be Cloned)**

> Assume our feature set ships in a competitor's release by Friday — realistic, in this market. What they don't get: our eval suite of 900 real resolutions, which took two years of closed tickets to build and is why our agent knows when *not* to act. And they don't get our refusal: prospects keep asking us to fall back to a human silently when the agent is unsure. We won't — the agent says "I'm handing you to a person" out loud, because trust is the product. That refusal costs us deals every month. It's also why our tribe evangelizes.

**What changed:** The moat is stated as taste (the refusal, with its ongoing cost) plus systems (the eval suite as accumulated, unclonable judgment). The interview question that produced it: "where was the cheap workaround available, and why did you refuse it?"

## 5. The Character, Not the Logo Parade

**Founder's material:** "Team slide: our backgrounds."

**❌ The old way (Team slide)**

> Jane Doe, CEO — ex-Google, Stanford MBA. John Smith, CTO — ex-Meta, 15 years in ML. Advisors: [four headshots of well-known names].

**Problems:**

- Logos say where people sat, not what they saw. Ex-Google describes ten thousand people a year.
- Nothing explains why *this* team is inevitable for *this* problem — the only question the slide exists to answer.
- Advisor headshots are borrowed credibility, and read that way.

**✅ After pitch-story (The Character, and the bar)**

> Jane ran a 40-person support org before she could code — she spent three years as the human fallback for a "seamless AI" vendor, and Corely is built out of everything that vendor got wrong. John left Meta's ranking team because he wanted models whose mistakes he'd have to answer for by name. We've made four hires and passed on nine — including a brilliant ex-DeepMind researcher who wanted to ship the silent-fallback hack. The engineering seat he'd have filled has been open five months. The bar is the bar.

**What changed:** The interview asked "what did your life force you to see?" and "who did you pass on, and what did it cost?" The wound explains the company; the pass proves the standard. This is the team slide investors actually wish they were getting.

## Anti-Patterns Summary

| Theme | Anti-Pattern | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Opinion | Agreeable mission statement in borrowed language | One belief a smart person would argue with |
| The Tribe | TAM arithmetic, "just 2%" | Who found you unsold, quoted, and how they resemble each other |
| Technical Depth | Feature grid, unsourced "10x" claims | One decision with its cost, one rejected architecture, one honest eval |
| The Moat | 2x2 with yourself top-right | Assume cloned by Friday; what remains is taste + systems |
| The Team | Logo parade, advisor headshots | What their life forced them to see; the hire they passed on |

## Key Insight

The old slides aren't stupid — they follow the best practices of their era, exactly as the overengineered code examples follow design patterns. The problem is **timing**: the Sequoia template was built for a world where building was expensive, so a credible *plan* was the scarce signal. Investors funded plans.

Building is cheap now. Plans are the most clonable artifact in the room — a competent founder can generate a full Sequoia-format deck in an afternoon, and investors know it. What can't be generated is a decision that already cost something, an eval built from two years of real tickets, a refusal that loses deals every month, a tribe quoting you unprompted.

**A good pitch shows decisions that cost something, not plans that promise everything.** The old deck asked investors to believe a story about the future. The new one shows fingerprints from a story already being lived — and lets investors conclude the future themselves.
