# Line Editing: the anti-slop pass

Load this before drafting any prose in Phase 5, and again in Phase 7. Run it on the memo, the one-pager, the cold email, and every chapter line. The story graph makes the pitch true. This pass makes it read the way a person writes.

A base model has a house style, and investors have read a thousand documents in it. The tells below are how a reader knows a machine wrote the draft. Cut them.

## The banned constructions

These are reflexes. Kill them on sight.

1. **The antithesis — "not X, but Y" / "it's not X, it's Y" / "more than just X."** The loudest AI tell there is. It fakes insight by pairing a strawman with a claim. Keep a contrast only when both halves carry real weight and the reader needs the first to understand the second; then write it plainly and once. Default: state the point and delete the strawman.
   - Slop: "We don't sell software, we sell outcomes."
   - Sharp: "Customers pay us per resolved ticket."
2. **The triad.** Three items in a rhythmic cadence — "fast, cheap, and reliable," "we build, we ship, we learn." One real item beats three padded ones. If you have three, make each earn its place or cut to two.
3. **The em-dash pile-up.** More than one em-dash in a paragraph reads as a model catching its breath. Budget: one per paragraph at most, zero in a headline. A period usually does the job.
4. **The hollow intensifier.** "truly," "deeply," "incredibly," "seamless," "robust," "powerful," "world-class," "cutting-edge," "game-changing." Delete every one. If the thing is powerful, a number shows it.
5. **The abstraction opener.** "In a world where…," "At its core…," "The truth is…," "Here's the thing," "Imagine a…." Start with the fact instead.
6. **The participial trail.** Sentences that end by stacking "-ing" consequences: "…, driving growth, enabling teams, unlocking value." Make the claim, then stop.
7. **The formula cadence.** "By doing X, you get Y." "What if you could…." "Enter [product]." Marketing rhythm reads as marketing. Say what happened.

## Adjectives: spend them like money

- Verbs and nouns carry a sentence. An adjective is interest on a loan you may not repay.
- One adjective per sentence, at most. Zero is often stronger.
- Never stack two on one noun ("a powerful, intuitive platform").
- Where a fact exists, use it instead of the adjective. "Fast" becomes "answers in 200ms." "Trusted" becomes "renewed by 9 of their first 10." "Scalable" becomes "same latency at 40× the load."
- If you cannot swap the adjective for a fact, you may not have the fact. Send it to the gap list.

## Rhythm and cut

- One idea per sentence. Two "and"s usually mean two sentences.
- Vary length. A model writes every sentence the same length; a person does not. Put a short sentence after a long one.
- Cut hedges: "somewhat," "fairly," "arguably," "essentially," "I think," "it seems."
- Cut throat-clearing: "It's worth noting that," "Importantly," "Interestingly," "Needless to say."
- Read it aloud. If you run out of breath, split it. If it sounds like a brochure, it is one.

## Before / after

| Slop (the model's default) | Sharp (a person wrote this) |
| --- | --- |
| "We're not just a database, we're a platform for the AI era." | "We store vectors and run the search in one query." |
| "Our powerful, intuitive tooling empowers developers to ship faster." | "A deploy that took our first customer 40 minutes now takes 90 seconds." |
| "In a world where data is exploding, observability has never been more critical." | "Their logging bill tripled in one quarter, and nobody could say which service did it." |
| "This isn't about features. It's about trust." | "They stayed after the outage. That is the number that matters." |
| "A seamless, end-to-end solution that scales with your needs." | "One binary. It runs the same on a laptop and on 200 nodes." |

## The self-check (run before delivering)

Scan the draft and count:

- "not X, but Y" and "it's not…it's" constructions. Target zero. Rewrite each, or justify why the contrast is load-bearing.
- em-dashes. One per paragraph at most, none in headlines.
- adjectives per sentence. Average under one.
- banned intensifiers and abstraction openers. Zero.

A clean draft reads plainer than the model wanted to write it. That plainness is the taste — a partner trusts a document that is not trying to sell them with its adverbs.
